The Australia LMI calculator estimates the Lenders Mortgage Insurance premium you may need to pay when your home-loan deposit is under 20%. It helps first-home buyers and investors understand this often-overlooked upfront cost so they can budget accurately and weigh the trade-off between buying sooner and saving a larger deposit.

How the Australia LMI Calculator Works

LMI is priced mainly on two things: your loan amount and your loan-to-value ratio (LVR), which is the loan divided by the property value. The calculator works out your LVR, then applies an indicative premium rate that rises as your LVR moves past the 80%, 85%, 90%, and 95% thresholds — because a smaller deposit means more risk for the insurer if you default.

The result is an estimate of the one-off premium, which is typically capitalised (added to your loan) and repaid with interest over the life of the mortgage. Because rates differ between insurers and lenders, treat the figure as a guide and confirm the exact premium with your lender before committing. Even so, seeing an approximate cost early makes it much easier to decide whether paying LMI to buy now is worth it, or whether saving a little longer will save you thousands.

Example 1: Buying a $600,000 home with a $60,000 (10%) deposit means a $540,000 loan at 90% LVR — a high-LVR band where LMI can run into several thousand dollars.
Example 2: Saving a $120,000 (20%) deposit on the same $600,000 home drops your LVR to 80%, which usually means no LMI is payable at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Lenders Mortgage Insurance (LMI) in Australia?

LMI is a one-off insurance premium that protects the lender — not the borrower — if you default on your home loan. Australian lenders generally require it when you borrow more than 80% of the property's value.

When do I have to pay LMI?

LMI usually applies when your deposit is less than 20% of the purchase price, meaning your loan-to-value ratio (LVR) is above 80%. The higher your LVR, the larger the premium tends to be.

How is LMI calculated?

LMI is based mainly on your loan amount and your loan-to-value ratio. Premiums rise sharply as LVR climbs past 85%, 90% and 95%, and larger loans attract higher rates. Insurers also consider whether you are an owner-occupier or investor.

Can I avoid paying LMI?

Yes — the common ways are saving a 20% deposit, using a guarantor, or qualifying for a government scheme or a lender waiver available to some professions. Check current eligibility rules, as schemes change over time.

Is the LMI premium refundable?

LMI is generally non-refundable once the loan settles, though a partial refund may be available if you repay the loan very early. Policies vary between insurers, so confirm the terms with your lender.
